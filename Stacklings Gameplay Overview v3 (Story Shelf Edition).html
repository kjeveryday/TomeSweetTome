808.3 STA

Vision

Stacklings is a monster-collecting-and-care game for readers aged 6–12 in which every creature is born from a real library book. A child borrows a book, and somewhere in that act — a barcode scan, a patterned egg, a shy wild thing haunting the pages — a specific creature comes to them, and the same book yields the same creature for every child, everywhere. Reading is the game's invisible engine: minutes with a book hatch, feed, and grow the collection, while parents and librarians act as facilitators rather than gatekeepers. Over time, the player's private Story Shelf becomes a memory of what they have read and an explainable portrait of what they tend to love; with explicit consent, that taste can power safe book recommendations and creature visits without exposing a public reading history. Stacklings is also defined by its refusals. It is not a store: library books are the only input, and there are no purchases or paid collectibles, ever. It is not a Tamagotchi with teeth, because creatures never die, never visibly suffer, and forgive every absence. Nor is it homework — no grades, no quizzes, no shame for the slow reader or the stalled book — and it is not a social network: minimal PII, no open feeds, COPPA-conscious throughout. The game succeeds by exactly one measure — kids read more, and love it.

025.3 USE

How to use this document

This manuscript unifies five module design documents — and, in this edition, the approved portions of a second, independently produced design study — into one decision package, then adds a seventh module developed from the Story Shelf brief. Each core module — Capture, Care, Gameplay Loop, Reading Gamification, and Library Systems — is presented with fully worked alternatives: three per module, plus a fourth capture alternative admitted from the second study. Two optional modules follow: Shared Play (§10), which creates cooperative rooms, and Story Shelf & Book Personality (§11), which records the player's reading life and can power consent-based recommendations. Both can be skipped without breaking Modules 1–5, although Story Shelf A is recommended as a foundational account feature. Because every alternative communicates with the rest of the game only through the shared event contract in §4, any combination of one alternative per module produces a functional game: no alternative may require a specific choice elsewhere, only prefer one, and each states its degraded-but-functional behavior with any other pairing. The executive and the game designer should read each module's design tensions, weigh the alternatives (each includes mechanics, worked examples with real books and real numbers, strengths and risks, and compatibility notes), and select one per module. Section 12 offers a compatibility matrix and four named reference builds as starting points — the fourth is the second study's own bundle, preserved as an argument rather than an endorsement; Section 13 consolidates every design tension into the workshop's decision agenda; Appendices A–E hold what applies to every build regardless of the choices made: the technical envelope, the privacy and data envelope, a candidate bestiary, the pilot plan with pre-committed kill criteria, and the program-level risk register. Each module closes with a recommendation signal that reports what the evidence or design logic favors without making the decision, and with acceptance criteria that any chosen alternative must pass.

One editorial note: voice and vocabulary have been unified across the source documents. Verification levels everywhere use the gamification module's ladder (§4), and creature genre vocabulary is normalized to the eight-element table (§4). Where two sources genuinely contradict each other, both claims are preserved and marked ⚑ RECONCILE… — there are twelve such flags, nine from the original unification and three born in this merge, and each belongs on the workshop agenda. A second attribution rule governs this edition: material admitted from the second design study is credited where it lands ("proposed in the second design study"), and where its claims could not be grounded in the five cited research reports, they are presented as that study's design judgment — never as research fact, and never carrying a [source] tag.

027.62 PIL

The four pillars

  1. Library books are the input, venue, and partner. Free and kid-safe; no purchases, no paid collectibles, ever.
  2. Collecting is deterministic. Same book → same creature everywhere. That determinism holds is a pillar; how it is achieved is an open design choice.
  3. Care is cozy, never punishing. Creatures never die or visibly suffer from absence; no timer can ever be bought out, because there is no money.
  4. Reading is the invisible engine. The game succeeds when kids read more; it must never feel like homework, and rewards stay reading-adjacent — the research is clear that literacy-related rewards preserve intrinsic motivation while junk rewards undermine it.
005.71 API

The shared interface contract

The seven modules (Shared Play and Story Shelf are optional) are interchangeable because they talk to each other only through a small set of standard events — a small, fixed vocabulary of things that happen in the game. A library alternative announces that a real borrow occurred; a gamification alternative announces that reading happened and how confident we are that it did; a capture alternative announces that a book resolved into a creature; care and loop alternatives announce care actions, growth, and appointment hooks; Story Shelf converts those moments into a private book record and a coarse taste profile. Any alternative may listen to any event and may prefer richer ones, but must function on the minimum set. This is what lets the workshop mix and match: the plumbing never changes, only the rooms.

The first ten events are the minimum vocabulary every alternative must function on. The last seven are optional: only Library B or C can emit holds and renewals, only a build that ships Module 6 emits contribution receipts, and only a build that ships Module 7 emits shelf, reaction, personality, and recommendation events, so every consumer must degrade gracefully when they never arrive. The receipt's fixed normalized_value: 1 is load-bearing — a five-minute shared read, a long solo session, a branch visit, and a checkout each yield exactly one receipt, which is what keeps shared progress from ever becoming a reading-volume leaderboard. The Story Shelf events carry canonical book tokens and coarse facets across modules, never public child profiles, raw circulation records, private notes, or a reconstructable taste history.

Shared vocabularies, normalized game-wide

Elements. A fixed table maps children's genre headings (LCGFT and Children's Subject Headings — the library world's own classifications) to eight creature elements: Wisp (fantasy), Fuzz (animal stories), Cog (nonfiction/STEM), Giggle (humor), Shade (mystery), Chime (poetry/rhyme), Hush (bedtime/picture books), and Ember (adventure/mythology). Species families nest under elements — Gigglekin, Impkin, and Snortling under Giggle; Kindred and Weaverkin under Fuzz; Wispling under Wisp; Hushling and Sproutling under Hush; and so on — so every module keys creatures, care needs, and rewards off the same genre spine. ⚑ RECONCILEthe eight-element table has no home for science fiction (the Cosmoblot family in the library module's Zita the Spacegirl example) or for scary stories (the "Spooky" genre in Care C's Shivers example) — extend the table or formally assign these headings to existing elements before content production begins. Candidate answers from the second design study — Gearling for science fiction, a safe-spooky family, and four more — are staged in Appendix C.

Verification. ReadingSessionLogged and BookFinished carry a verification_level from the gamification module's ladder, used consistently throughout this manuscript: honor (self-reported), co_signed (an adult confirmed), timed (device-timed session), timed_scanned (timed with the physical book scanned in hand), and checkpointed (story-consistent checkpoint answers). Downstream modules scale enrichment to confidence, never access — an honor-level session always earns baseline value. Borrowing events carry a separate provenance signal from the library module, three-valued since the merge: self_reported, receipt_verified (a library receipt QR, rotating branch event code, or staff token — it proves an event occurred, not identity or history; proposed in the second design study, detailed in §9 A), and ils_verified. Where the source modules said "unverified," this manuscript says honor-level.

Presentation profiles. One collection, multiple presentations: a profile is a lens, never a fork. Co-reader (6–7) gets large icons, spoken text, and one-tap feelings, drawings, or voice notes in place of typed reflection; Explorer (8–10) is the default; Curator (11–12) gets denser UI, privacy controls, and private notes and shelf lists. Profiles alter copy, assistance, density, and optional depth — never species identity, reward value, or access to the core fantasy. 793.932 CAPMODULE 1

Module 1 — Capture: how a book becomes a Stackling

This module defines the pipeline from a physical library book to a specific, deterministic creature, and the player act that acquires it. It owns the generation algorithm (what seeds species, traits, and rarity), the capture ritual (scan, incubation, or minigame), duplicate and re-scan policy, offline behavior, and the edge cases: books with no ISBN, library-sticker barcodes, siblings sharing one copy, and non-book items. It emits CreatureGenerated and CreatureAcquired; everything downstream consumes what this module produces.

Design tensions. Five decisions shape this module. First, reward the scan or the read: instant hatch-at-shelf maximizes the magic moment but pays out before any reading happens, while hatch-gated-by-reading serves Pillar 4 but delays the payoff for slow or struggling readers. The second design study answers with a third verb — reward the borrow — splitting the payoff between an instant sighting at the shelf and residency at the checkout desk (Alternative D below). Second, where determinism lives: a pure barcode hash works forever offline with zero infrastructure physical_capture_report, Skannerz but produces creatures blind to what the book is about, while metadata-driven generation makes creatures thematically true but needs catalog connectivity and a maintained authority mapping. Third, the edition problem: is Charlotte's Web one creature or one per ISBN? Per-edition hashing creates Monster-Rancher-style pressing-hunting monster_generation_mechanics_report §2.2 — a community delight for older kids, a fairness landmine for younger ones comparing across libraries. Fourth, duplicate policy: grinding duplicates into currency (the Pokémon GO stardust model) keeps the verb valuable but can make re-reading feel transactional; treating duplicates as pure sentiment risks re-reads feeling unrewarded. Fifth, the friction budget: every ritual step — mic, timing tap, page scan — is charm for a 10-year-old and a wall for a pre-reader on a shared family device, so each alternative must state its assist mode.

Generation inputs beyond genre (all metadata-driven alternatives). The second design study contributes a set of catalog signals the five source modules never touched, usable by Alternatives B, C, and D at generation time and by A at metadata sync:

ASpine Spark — every barcode is an egg

How it works

Spine Spark is the Skannerz lineage played straight: scanning a book's ISBN barcode instantly hatches a creature, deterministically, with no server required physical_capture_report §2. The generation function is a pure hash: the app normalizes the barcode to ISBN-13, computes SHA-256 over the 13 digits, and reads the creature out of the bytes the way Pokémon reads traits out of a PID monster_generation_mechanics_report §3: one number, many correlated traits, zero stored data.

The mapping is structured so the visible parts of the barcode mean something — the piece of Skannerz worth stealing, because kids can learn that the digits themselves carry clues. The ISBN's registrant element (the publisher prefix) selects one of 24 Ink Clans: every HarperCollins book hatches a Growl Clan creature, every Scholastic book a Zoom Clan creature. This is the tribe system without the tribe-lock — no content is hardware-gated, because Pillar 1 forbids purchases. Within the clan, hash bytes select species (from a 480-species table), a temperament (byte mod 8), a size class (byte mod 5), and three cozy stats (Pep, Snuggle, Curiosity, each 3–10). One rarity byte gives a flat 1/128 "Gilded" shimmer — fixed odds, no pity timers, no manipulation surface.

The capture act is a three-second ceremony, not a raw scan: the camera frames the barcode, the stripes animate into a zipper, the zipper opens, and the creature pops out drowsy and yawning. Drowsy is the reading hook: a Spine Spark hatchling arrives at 30% energy and only fully wakes after its book logs one ReadingSessionLogged. Scanning without reading fills the shelf with napping creatures — visible, collectible, but inert — which keeps the loop honest without punishing anyone.

Rules and edge cases. Only 978/979 (Bookland) barcodes resolve; a cereal box or DVD gets a gentle "no story sleeping in there." Library item stickers (the 14-digit Codabar barcode on the cover) are recognized and resolved to the book's ISBN via the ILS when online; offline, the app prompts "find the barcode on the back of the book." Books with no barcode at all (old rebinds) use the Title Whisper: type or voice-enter title + author, normalized (uppercase, punctuation stripped) and fed to the same hash — the Monster Rancher Advance password precedent monster_generation_mechanics_report §2.4. Re-scans of an already-hatched book always show the same creature waving hello: the first scan per book per account acquires; later scans are greetings, so shelf strip-mining yields nothing after scan one. Rate cap: 5 hatches per day, refreshed at the library (any BookCheckedOut adds +2), so Barcode Battler's grocery-store-scanning failure mode never appears. Siblings scanning the same copy each hatch the identical species — determinism is the pillar — but bond level, nickname, and earned bond-charms are per-account, so "mine" still feels like mine. One Easter egg, straight homage to Radica's self-healing prefix physical_capture_report §2.1: scanning your own library card once per day dispenses a Library Snack for the care module.

Offline behavior is Spine Spark's superpower: the entire pipeline is client-side math, so a kid in a library basement with no signal hatches identically to everyone else. Genre affinity is the one deferred piece — the hash cannot know the book is a mystery — so the creature hatches element-neutral and soaks up its element when metadata arrives with the next sync or BookCheckedOut event: a visible, charming "your creature turned out to be a Shade type!" moment rather than a silent patch.

BStory Eggs — hatched by reading

How it works

Story Eggs inverts the payout: the scan gives you an egg; the reading gives you the creature. Determinism lives entirely in library metadata, not the barcode — which solves the edition problem outright, because generation keys off a work-level record (title + author normalized to a work ID, the same clustering librarians already rely on). The 1952 hardcover and the 2012 paperback of Charlotte's Web hatch the same creature in every library in the country.

Generation is a readable recipe over the catalog record, in the spirit of Monster Rancher's TOC parse but with meaningful fields monster_generation_mechanics_report §2.2, §11.1 — "the input is the fantasy". The genre heading picks the element and species family; up to three subject headings each map to a trait from a curated 300-entry trait dictionary ("Spiders—Fiction" → Weaver; "Schools—Fiction" → Classmate); page count sets size class and the hatch threshold; a publication date older than 50 years grants the Vintage trait (sepia egg speckles); a Newbery or Caldecott medal in the record grants the Laureate shimmer (silver or gold trim) — rarity earned by the book's real-world honors rather than a dice roll. A final tiebreak hash over the work ID picks among species candidates so every book still resolves uniquely. Rarity therefore has three honest tiers: common (most books), Vintage (~15% of a typical children's collection), and Laureate (a few hundred titles ever); kids can hunt rarity by asking a librarian for award winners, which is precisely the behavior we want to cause.

The ritual: at BookCheckedOut or BookScanned, the egg appears in the nest, patterned with hints (web filigree, a gold medal stamp) that seed guessing games. The egg's hatch meter fills from ReadingSessionLogged minutes, scaled to the book: threshold = pages × 0.9 minutes, clamped to [15, 300]. A picture book hatches in one bedtime; a middle-grade novel takes a few honest evenings. Hatching is deliberately allowed before finishing — kids abandon books, and an egg that hostage-holds a creature behind BookFinished turns reading into homework, violating Pillar 4's spirit. Instead, BookFinished grants a bonus Finisher trait and a hatch-day cutscene where the creature reenacts a moment from its subject headings. BookReturned(on_time: true) while an egg is unhatched converts it to a dormant egg that keeps its progress forever and resumes if the book is ever borrowed again — nothing is lost, per Pillar 3.

Edge cases. Offline scan: the app caches catalog records for everything in the kid's current checkout stack at each sync; a scan of an unknown book offline produces a Mystery Egg (plain white, still accruing reading minutes) that resolves species on the next sync — graceful degradation, the same zeros-for-missing-values move Monster Rancher shipped with monster_generation_mechanics_report §2.2. No ISBN or no barcode: irrelevant — title/author lookup reaches the same work record, so the barcode is only ever a convenience. Non-book items: no record, no egg, gentle message. Siblings: same work → same species for both, but each account's egg incubates on that account's own reading; the twin creatures are identical species with per-account bonds and nicknames. Re-reads of an already-hatched book route sessions to the living creature as Echo Visits — growth food for the care module rather than a second egg. Duplicates are nourishment here, which keeps re-reading warm instead of grindy.

CThe Lantern Ritual — befriending the book-wild

How it works

The Lantern Ritual makes capture an act the kid performs with the physical book open in their lap — a 60-second bedtime ceremony, descended from Pokémon GO's two-stage encounter/catch split physical_capture_report §4.1 but rebuilt cozy: probability tension without loss.

Generation is hybrid, taking the best half of each prior alternative. When a book enters play (BookCheckedOut or first BookScanned), metadata picks the family — genre heading → element → one of 8–14 species families — and an ISBN-13 hash mints the individual: a 32-bit Stackling ID, our PID monster_generation_mechanics_report §3.1. The SID selects species within the family (mod family size), a nature (mod 12: Dozy, Bold, Sheepish, …), and — the showpiece — Spinda-style procedural markings: four SID bytes place four freckles or patches on the creature's body via nibble coordinates monster_generation_mechanics_report §3.2, so every book's creature has a distinctive, deterministic coat that kids can compare ("your Hatchet one has the eye-patch!"). SID mod 100 == 0 → Luminous variant (1/100, softly glowing). Same book, same edition → same creature, same freckles, everywhere. If metadata is unavailable (offline, or no-ISBN via typed Title Whisper), the hash alone runs and family assignment arrives on sync — full offline function with deferred flavor.

The wild creature then haunts the book: it peeks from the app's nest screen, shy and uncaught, for as long as the book is checked out. To befriend it, the kid performs the Lantern Ritual, ideally at bedtime with the physical book. (1) Show it home — point the camera at any open page; a page-shaped glow confirms (no OCR needed, a paper-texture check suffices; tap-fallback if no camera). (2) Say the word — the app displays the creature's summon word, always a real word drawn from the book's title ("wild," "goodnight," "web"); the kid whispers it into the mic, or taps each letter as it's read aloud — the pre-reader/mic-off mode, quietly a phonics touch. (3) Catch the glow — one gentle timing tap as a lantern ring pulses; the window is generous, and a miss makes the creature giggle and the ring pulse again. Nothing here can fail permanently.

What can happen is "not yet." Settle chance starts at 35% with zero minutes read and climbs with ReadingSessionLogged minutes on that book — 35% → 80% at 60 minutes → guaranteed at 180 minutes (picture books: any single logged session → guaranteed). An unsettled creature scampers off leaving an inky footprint in the kid's journal — a collection hint and a nudge to read tonight and try again tomorrow. One ritual attempt per book per day creates the appointment rather than gating it. Two failsafes keep this pillar-safe: BookFinished auto-settles instantly, and at BookReturned, any book with at least one logged session has its creature follow the kid home automatically — no child ever loses a creature to skill or schedule. Ritual performance never touches species or traits (determinism preserved); a first-try settle awards a cosmetic Bond Spark charm only.

Duplicates and siblings: re-borrowing a settled book lets the kid re-run the ritual as a Reunion (a care-module treat, no second creature). Two siblings, one copy, one shared device: profiles switch at ritual start; both befriend the identical species and freckles, each with their own bond — and the ritual is genuinely fun to perform for an audience, the shared-bedroom demo scene. Non-book barcodes: "no story sleeping in there." Library card scan: daily Library Snack, as in Alternative A.

DThe Standing Invitation — sighting at the shelf, residency at the desk (proposed in the second design study)

How it works

The Standing Invitation splits acquisition into two stages so that the library transaction itself becomes the acquisition verb. Any valid scan instantly reveals the book's creature as a Sighting: a stable, deterministic reveal in under a second, fully inspectable — the kid can watch it idle, learn its likely preferences, add it as a shadow entry to a Field Guide, wish-list it, or ask an adult to place a hold (which, where the library module emits HoldPlaced, spawns the traveling message egg described in §9 B). What a scan alone never does is move the creature in. A second signal — the borrow — converts the Sighting into a Resident of the habitat: BookCheckedOut at any provenance qualifies, meaning an ils_verified sync event, a receipt_verified QR scan at the desk (§9 A), or, in the fully degraded mode, a player-confirmed "I borrowed this!" at self_reported. Reading then builds the bond exactly as the chosen care module defines it, and an on-time BookReturned grants a permanent Archive Mark — the creature "graduates" into the collection forever, wearing the mark. A late return still graduates, just without the bonus stamp; nothing scolds, per Pillar 3.

Where the other three alternatives pay at the scan (A) or in reading minutes (B, C), D pays at the borrow. The instant reveal keeps A's shelf magic intact while structurally blunting shelf strip-mining: an afternoon of scanning fills the Field Guide with sightings — a real and pleasant collection layer in its own right — but the habitat grows only by walking books to the desk. D is a capture architecture, not a generation recipe: the sighting's determinism can run on A's pure hash or C's hybrid, whichever the workshop selects, provided the reveal works offline in under a second.

Duplicates and edge cases. Re-scanning a Resident's book is a greeting, as in A. A sighting with no borrow persists indefinitely in the Field Guide and converts whenever a borrow eventually happens. Siblings each convert their own residency from the same family checkout, with per-account bonds and nicknames. Books that can't be borrowed — owned books, little-free-library finds — convert by the player-confirmed route, so no child is locked out of residency by lacking a card, though the provenance signal stays honest about the difference.

Acceptance criteria (any alternative)

Adapted from the second design study's per-module acceptance tests, recast so that none presupposes a specific alternative; the workshop's chosen capture design must pass all of these.

  1. The same seed version and canonical identifier produce the same base species on mobile and web across three consecutive runs.
  2. A valid ISBN produces its deterministic result without internet access; metadata may enrich later.
  3. No title yields a stronger creature because it is longer, harder, newer, or more expensive.
  4. Metadata failure changes enrichment quality, never the ability to capture.

636.08 CARMODULE 2

Module 2 — Care: what a Stackling needs (and never needs)

This module owns everything a player does with a Stackling after it enters the collection: feeding, bonding, habitats, moods, growth and evolution, and — critically — what the game does when the player is away. Care resources flow exclusively from reading and borrowing events, keyed to library genre classifications, so that caring for a creature is always, invisibly, a reason to read.

Design tensions. The central choice is breadth versus depth of attachment: a colony model spreads affection across dozens of creatures and scales with borrowing volume, while a single-companion model produces the strongest emotional bond but risks making the rest of the collection feel like furniture. Screen time cuts against reading time: the richer the in-app care loop, the more it competes with the very reading it is supposed to cause, so every alternative states how it caps session length — and the executive decides how aggressive that cap is. Care economies that pay out per reading minute lean on the gamification module's verification_level; the executive must decide whether honor-level reading earns full, partial, or cosmetic-only care resources. There is also the question of what "hunger" means in a game where nothing bad happens — each alternative softens the need-state differently (curiosity, sleepiness, story-appetite), and the choice determines whether kids feel invited back or merely unpunished. The second design study offers a candidate vocabulary any alternative could adopt: five states that are all invitations — Resting, Curious, Inspired, Ready to Grow, Celebrating — each with a cause, a visible cue, and an optional action, none a deficit; reading can move a creature from Curious to Inspired, but not reading never moves it anywhere harmful. Finally, book return is an emotional event: when a source book goes back to the library, the creature's relationship must never feel like loss, and each alternative resolves this differently — the executive should pick one framing and enforce it game-wide.

One question none of the five source modules asked: what does the shelf feel like at creature two hundred? The second design study's answer is worth carrying into every alternative — curation, not accumulation, as the endgame. A limited active roster over an unlimited archive keeps big collections legible; duplicates become annotations, provenance variants, or pages in a keepsake Folio, never upgrade fodder; and progression stays horizontal, so new creatures create new combinations without invalidating old favorites. Whichever care model wins, the workshop should put this abundance question to it explicitly (the full risk entry is Appendix E, #15).

AThe Stackyard — a habitat colony (the Neko Atsume model)

How it works

The Stackyard is a single, lovingly detailed diorama — a library corner after closing time, with shelves, a window seat, a reading lamp, and a rug — that the player decorates and provisions, then leaves. Creatures are not pets you hold; they are neighbors you host. The core verbs: set out snacks, place furniture, close the app, and come back later to discover who visited, what they did, and what they left behind. The template is Neko Atsume's inverted pressure model, chosen deliberately because it is the clearest proof that a return loop needs no threat to function — neglect produces only an empty snack plate, never a sad creature cozy_game_return_loops_report.

Provisioning. Reading generates Snackplates, the only consumable. Every ReadingSessionLogged converts minutes into genre-flavored snacks at a flat rate: 10 verified minutes = 1 snack of the book's genre; honor-level minutes earn snacks at half rate — the tuning knob for the verification tension above. A BookFinished produces one Feast, a premium plate of that book's genre. BookCheckedOut grants one free Sampler plate of the new book's genre immediately, so a library trip always stocks the yard the same day. The yard holds up to 4 plates at once; each plate lasts 8 real-time hours or until eaten. Genre is the matchmaking system: Animal-fiction snacks attract animal-book Stacklings, Spooky snacks attract creatures born from scary stories, and so on, using the shared genre headings.

Visits and residency. While the app is closed, owned Stacklings (and occasional un-captured "wanderers" — see compatibility notes) visit on hidden timers: a visit roll every 2–4 real hours per active plate, weighted toward creatures whose genre matches the food. Returning players find a Yard Report: snapshots of who came, little vignettes (two creatures sharing a beanbag; one asleep inside a bookend), and Mementos — small thank-you gifts (a pressed leaf, a lost button, a bookmark charm) that fill a keepsake album. After 5 visits a creature becomes a Resident: it appears in the yard live, has a favorite spot, and can be petted, photographed, and nicknamed. Residency is the growth system: Residents advance through three visible comfort stages — Perching → Nesting → Home at 5, 15, and 35 cumulative visits — each adding idle animations and one physical flourish (a scarf, rounder cheeks, a tiny stack of books it "collects").

Furniture and expansion. Furniture is crafted from Bindings, a material earned only by BookReturned(on_time: true) (2 Bindings) or any BookFinished (1 Binding) — returning books on time is how the yard grows. Each furniture piece is genre-tagged (a cardboard rocket for Science nonfiction; a toadstool stool for Fairy tales) and raises visit odds for that genre by 25%. The yard expands twice — a Window Seat annex at 20 total Mementos, a Rooftop Garden at 60 — giving the long-arc collection goal.

Absence. Nothing decays. An empty yard simply pauses; on return after any gap, the game plays a "the yard missed you" beat — Residents wave from their spots and the oldest Resident has saved the player one Memento. Session shape is intentionally short: provision, read the Yard Report, take a photo, done in 3–5 minutes, with the game explicitly saying "the yard is set — see you after your next chapter." Clean stopping points and honest time-gates are the two properties the return-loop research most strongly correlates with player affection cozy_game_return_loops_report.

BPocket Familiar — one deep bond (a gentle Chao model)

How it works

One Stackling at a time is the player's Familiar — carried on the home screen, greeted by name, and raised through a genuine (but entirely kind) hidden-variable growth system in the Chao Garden / Digimon World lineage: accumulated care silently routes the creature down branching growth paths, so who your monster becomes is the consequence of your relationship with it monster_generation_mechanics_report. The rest of the collection lives in the Dormitory Shelf, a visible cutaway bookshelf where non-active creatures nap in cozy book-nook bedrooms. Shelved creatures are in stasis-comfort: they never need anything, and swapping the active Familiar is free and instant, once per day, to protect against decision paralysis.

Daily care ritual. The Familiar has three gentle, non-decaying meters — Tummy, Sparkle, Snuggle — displayed as expressions, never numbers. Each is filled by one short action per day: feeding a Morsel (genre-flavored, generated at 10 verified reading minutes = 1 Morsel, capped at 4/day to bound the loop), a two-minute play interaction (a page-flip peekaboo minigame; no skill requirement), and a bedtime tuck-in that closes the session ("Pip is dreaming — see you tomorrow"). Full meters do nothing but make the creature visibly delighted; empty meters make it merely calm and a little sleepy. There is no bad state, which is the modern virtual-pet consensus the return-loop research documents: reward routine with growth and unlocks, never threaten loss — "watch it thrive," not "keep it alive" cozy_game_return_loops_report.

Hidden growth variables. Under the hood, each Familiar accumulates only positive counters — no care-mistake counter exists, deliberately inverting Digimon World's punitive bookkeeping while keeping its magic: identity as accumulated history monster_generation_mechanics_report. The tracked variables: Genre Nutrition (a running tally of Morsel genres fed), Feast Count (each BookFinished while this Familiar is active = 1 Feast, a special shared meal), Ritual Streak-Ever (a lifetime count of completed bedtime tuck-ins — a total, never a breakable streak), and Outing Count (times the Familiar was "brought along" on a BookScanned library visit). Growth stages: Inkling → Fledgling → Fullbound. Inkling→Fledgling fires at 3 Feasts OR 21 lifetime tuck-ins, whichever comes first — so both finishers and slow-and-steady readers evolve. Fledgling→Fullbound fires at 8 Feasts, and the form branches on dominant Genre Nutrition: a Familiar fed mostly Fantasy Morsels grows different features than one fed Nonfiction, echoing Chao alignment drift, where what you feed it becomes what it is monster_generation_mechanics_report. A secondary cosmetic layer — Marginalia: freckles, star-flecks, tiny reading glasses — unlocks from the minority genres, so eclectic reading always leaves a visible mark.

Absence and dreams. When the player is away more than 36 hours, the Familiar simply sleeps — and dreams about the player's last book. On return there is no apology screen; instead the Familiar excitedly shares a Dream Journal page: a charming illustrated non-sequitur built from the genre of the last-read book ("I dreamed we were on a boat made of tacos. You were there!"). Long absences generate one saved journal page per week, max 3, turning guilt into a small welcome-home gift — the "absence is forgiven and converted to anticipation" property the research identifies as the sharpest break from original Tamagotchi cozy_game_return_loops_report.

Edge cases. Returning the source book to the library changes nothing about the bond — the creature is yours; the book was its egg. If a player never swaps Familiars, shelf creatures periodically send tiny letters to the active one, gently advertising themselves without demanding anything.

CThe Read-Along — the creature as reading companion

How it works

In the Read-Along model, care is reading, performed in the same sitting. Each Stackling bonds to the player's currently open book (normally its own source book, but any borrowed book works — see edge cases), and its growth mirrors the player's literal progress through that book. The creature appears beside the in-app reading timer as a lap companion: it settles in when a session starts, reacts to the book's genre mood while you read (shivering happily during a Spooky book, wearing a paper helmet during an Adventure), and grows a visible amount when the session logs. The fantasy is a pet that reads over your shoulder — the input is the fantasy, the same design truth that made Monster Rancher's CD-reading feel like divination monster_generation_mechanics_report.

The session sandwich. A care session has three diegetic beats, totaling under 90 seconds of screen time wrapped around real off-screen reading. (1) Settle-in: the player opens the app, picks the book, and performs one small pre-reading action — fluff the cushion, light the reading lamp, share a pre-chapter snack (one tap each; pure charm, no economy). The app then dims to a quiet timer screen and gets out of the way. (2) The read: minutes accrue via the gamification module's session logging; the creature on the dimmed screen slowly relaxes, yawns, mimics page-turning. (3) The recap: when the session ends, the creature "asks" about the story with a single one-tap prompt — choose the face that matches the chapter (exciting / funny / sad / scary / cozy). The recap is the bonding mechanic: it is unverifiable and unscored on purpose, a 5-second act of telling your pet about your book, and it banks one Storythread in the book's genre color.

Growth in lockstep with the book. Each bonded creature has a Bookmark Meter that advances only from sessions with its bonded book; stage thresholds are set per book length from BookCheckedOut metadata. Standard tuning: picture books = 3 sessions to full growth; early chapter books = 5 sessions or ~90 logged minutes; middle-grade novels = 10 sessions or ~240 minutes. Stages are Sprout → Halfway Hero → Fullbound, with the mid-stage triggering at the meter's 50% mark and a visible costume or feature shift themed to the book's genre. BookFinished is the evolution ceremony regardless of meter state: the creature reaches Fullbound the moment the book is done, in a "The End" animation where it curls up inside the back cover and emerges transformed — finishing a book is never gated by care math. Storythreads from recaps are the persistent cosmetic economy: 5 threads of a genre weave one Story Scarf patch, a wearable patchwork that chronicles everything the pair has read together.

Absence and edge cases. Between sessions the creature simply holds your place — it is, diegetically, your bookmark, dozing at the exact chapter you stopped. Absence of any length means only a longer nap; on return it stretches, shows the page it "saved," and asks nothing. If the book is returned unfinished, the creature is not stranded: it keeps its current stage forever with a small "To Be Continued" ribbon, and re-borrowing the book any time later resumes the meter exactly — abandonment reframed as a bookmark, not a failure. A creature can also ride along with a different book than its source (kids will want their favorite on every book): it earns Storythreads normally but Bookmark Meter progress accrues at half rate, keeping a gentle pull toward hatching new creatures from new books. Multiple creatures may pile onto one long novel in "book club" mode (max 3), splitting no resources — everyone present gets full recap threads, because scarcity between your own pets would violate the cozy pillar.

Module 3 — Gameplay Loop: session shape, return cadence, library rhythm

This module defines the rhythm of Stacklings: what a single 5–10 minute session contains, what pulls a kid back tomorrow, and how the week-to-week cadence rides the real library loan cycle — borrow → read → return, on a 2–3 week clock. It also owns the ethics guardrails drawn directly from the cozy-loops research: return triggers framed as care or ritual, honest time-gating with no buyouts, engineered stopping points, and full absence forgiveness cozy_game_return_loops_report. Two further game-wide guardrails arrive from the second design study: notifications are opt-in and framed as invitations, never as warnings of loss, and no creature may ask a player to return during school or sleep hours. It does not own what creatures are or how they're captured or cared for; it owns when the game invites those things to happen.

Design tensions. What is the heartbeat? The daily anchor can be the creature's clock (Alternative A), the library trip (B), or the reading session itself (C) — and this decides whether a kid can meaningfully open the app on a day they don't read. How much game-without-reading is tolerable? The cozy research says to pad the daily wall with ungated play cozy_game_return_loops_report, but Pillar 4 caps how much non-reading play we can offer before the game competes with books. The due date is a real deadline in a game that forbids punishment; each alternative softens it differently (send-off ceremony, season finale, epilogue), and the executive must pick how much the loan clock is allowed to feel like a clock. On streaks, the research is clear that absence forgiveness drives goodwill, but the gamification module may want streak-like structures; this module recommends "chains that pause, never break" and needs a ruling. Finally, whose device and whose window: a 7-year-old on a shared family tablet has a parent-controlled evening window, while an 11-year-old with their own device self-schedules — Alternatives A and C assume very different device realities, and the executive should confirm the primary hardware model.

AThe Lamplight Hour — a care-ritual daily loop

How it works

The Lamplight Hour makes Stacklings a once-a-day care ritual in the direct lineage of Neko Atsume and Cozy Grove: a short, warm, self-limiting visit that ends by telling you to go cozy_game_return_loops_report. During family setup, a parent picks a daily "lamplight window" — typically 30–60 minutes around bedtime reading, e.g. 6:30–7:30 p.m. Stacklings are drowsy paper creatures; they wake only when the lamp comes on. Outside the window the den is a still, dim diorama you can look at but not poke. This is the honest time-gate: one visit a day, framed diegetically ("Stacklings keep library hours"), never bypassable, never monetized.

A session runs five to eight minutes with a fixed shape: greet → feed → tend → tuck in. Greeting each creature yields a one-line mood beat (consuming CreatureStateChanged). Feeding spends story crumbs, the only care currency, minted exclusively by reading — every ReadingSessionLogged converts minutes into crumbs at roughly 1 crumb per minute, and BookFinished mints a rare golden crumb. ⚑ RECONCILELoop A calls story crumbs "the only care currency" at ~1 crumb per verified minute, while Care A and B mint genre snacks/Morsels at 1 per 10 verified minutes; if the Lamplight Hour is paired with either care alternative, one currency and one conversion rate must win. Crumbs accrue silently all day from whenever reading was logged; the Lamplight Hour is when you spend them, which cleanly separates the reading moment (anytime, no screen required) from the game moment (short, scheduled). Tending is one small habitat touch per creature — a two-tap interaction the care module defines; this module only guarantees each creature surfaces at most one ask per day. Tucking in is the engineered stop: the kid dims the lamp, every creature yawns and curls into its book, and the screen literally says "See you tomorrow." There is nothing else to do. The session ends naturally, guilt-free, within eight minutes — the property reviewers single out in Cozy Grove and Hello Kitty Island Adventure cozy_game_return_loops_report.

The weekly layer rides the loan period. When BookCheckedOut fires, that book's creature arrives with a visible "visiting for 21 days" suitcase — the loan framed as a visit, not a countdown to loss. As the due date nears, PlayerReturnTrigger(reason: "walk_home_soon", window: due_date − 3d) prompts a "walking-home party": the family returns the book, BookReturned fires, and the creature's send-off is a small ceremony that leaves behind a memento (a photo in the den, a stamp — whatever the care and gamification modules define). On-time returns make the party slightly fancier; late returns are never scolded — the creature simply says "I stayed a few extra nights!"

Absence forgiveness is total: miss a night and the creatures are found napping, not neglected. Miss a month and the den is dusty (Animal Crossing's weeds — cosmetic, cleared in one tap) and every creature wakes delighted. Crumbs never expire. There are no streaks; there is a "lamplight chain" that pauses during absence and resumes where it left off. On a zero-crumb day (no reading logged), creatures still wake and can be greeted and tended — the ritual never locks out — but they gently mention they'd love a story tomorrow, a wish with no disappointment in it. Households with erratic evenings can set the window to "any one hour, once per day" instead of a fixed clock time.

BThe Borrowing Season — an expedition/collection loop

How it works

The Borrowing Season treats the loan period as the game's fundamental unit — a 2–3 week "season" that opens at the library and closes there — making the library trip the appointment the way Community Day is Pokémon GO's physical_capture_report. The library is the venue and the spawn table: the day-to-day game is deliberately quiet, and the place is loud.

A season opens on Expedition Day. At the library, the app enters Expedition Mode: a 15–20 minute assisted-browse session where scanning candidate books (BookScanned) shows a silhouette tease of the creature inside — enough to intrigue, never revealing the creature before checkout, preserving the pull-ritual tension that made barcode and CD capture compelling physical_capture_report. Checkout (BookCheckedOut) locks the stack: those 3–6 books become the season's expedition party, displayed as a base-camp tent of still-sleeping creatures. Creatures wake only through reading — each book's creature hatches at a genre-tuned reading threshold, consuming ReadingSessionLogged and BookFinished via the capture module's CreatureGenerated/CreatureAcquired.

Between library visits, the game intentionally shrinks. Mid-season sessions are 3–5 minute "camp checks," at most once daily: peek at hatch progress, one light camp chore, done. There is no daily content treadmill — the design accepts, on the strength of the Cozy Grove finding, that a self-limiting mid-week beats a bloated one, because the anticipation asset is the next library trip, not tomorrow's login cozy_game_return_loops_report. Two honest mid-season appointments punctuate the quiet: a Half-Moon Check at the season's midpoint (PlayerReturnTrigger(reason: "half_moon", window: day 10)), where camp creatures do a group scene sized to how much of the stack has hatched, and a Pack-Up Eve three days before the due date, framed as helping creatures pack souvenirs, so the due date approaches without ever sounding a klaxon.

Season close is Return Day: bring the stack back, BookReturned fires per book, and the season resolves into an Expedition Album page — a permanent, collectible record of that stack: which creatures hatched, a group photo, a stamp for on-time return. Unfinished books resolve kindly: their creatures appear on the page as "still dreaming," and re-borrowing the book later resumes the same hatch progress (determinism guarantees the same creature waits — Pillar 2). Nothing is lost, only postponed. Then the next Expedition Day opens the next season, usually the same visit — return and re-borrow in one trip, matching how families actually use libraries.

Absence forgiveness operates at season scale: skip a whole cycle and nothing rots — the album simply has no page for that fortnight, and the next visit opens a season like any other. There are no daily streaks at all; the only chain is album pages, which accumulate and never reset. Edge cases: kids without regular library access can open a "home season" from owned or school-library books via BookScanned — degraded but functional, with album pages styled differently so library seasons stay special; renewals extend the season honestly ("the expedition got a weather delay"); and mid-season checkouts add a straggler creature to camp.

CThe Story Keeper — a reading-session-anchored loop

How it works

The Story Keeper collapses the game session and the reading session into one object: you never "play Stacklings" — you read with your Stackling, and the game is a two-minute bookend on either side of the book. This is the most radical Pillar-4 design: screen time approaches zero, and the app's job is to make the creature the reason to open the book.

The session shape is settle → read → return → moment, 20–30 minutes end to end, of which under 3 are on-screen. Settle (45 seconds): open the app, pick tonight's book; the book's creature climbs out of it, curls up "to listen," and the screen inverts to a dim listening face with an optional gentle timer — then the device is meant to be put down or turned over. Read (15–25 minutes, off-screen): the actual reading, solo or read-aloud. Return (30 seconds): the kid closes the book, taps done, confirms roughly how far they got (emitting ReadingSessionLogged with whatever verification level the gamification module supplies). Moment (60–90 seconds): the payoff — the creature reacts to the session with a small variable-magnitude beat: usually a mood scene, sometimes a growth step (CreatureStateChanged), occasionally a surprise keyed to where you are in the book (a creature from a mystery gets twitchy near the reveal chapter). Then the creature yawns, crawls back into the book, and the session is over — the stopping point is structural, because the game has no other room to be in.

The daily return trigger is narrative hunger, handed to the creature: PlayerReturnTrigger(reason: "next_chapter", window: tomorrow) renders as "Emberquill wants to know what happens to Clay." That is the Zeigarnik pull of an unfinished story, transferred onto a creature who shares it — compulsion dressed as companionship, the reframe the cozy research treats as the difference between beloved and resented loops cozy_game_return_loops_report. One session per book per day counts fully; a second same-day session is welcomed but the creature is already content — honest pacing, no doubling.

The weekly cadence follows the loan mega-loop as chapters-per-loan math: the app quietly knows a 21-day loan and shows it as the creature's "visit," with a mid-loan "halfway through the visit" scene and, at BookFinished, an Epilogue Night — a longer 3-minute moment where the creature acts out its favorite scene and asks to be walked home. BookReturned closes with the creature waving from the book drop. Fast finishers get Epilogue Night on day 4 and a next-book suggestion; slow readers get renewals framed as "asking the library if Emberquill can stay longer."

Absence forgiveness has a mechanic all its own: return after any gap and the creature offers a "story so far" — a 20-second recap of where you left off in the book (sourced from the last logged position), which converts the classic lapsed-reader failure point ("I forgot what was happening, so I quit the book") into the warmest beat in the game. No streaks exist; the only counter is books-finished-together, which cannot go down. Edge cases: read-aloud households run Settle on the parent's device with the kid holding the book; audiobooks log as sessions if the library-systems module supports them; a kid juggling three books has three creatures each tracking their own story, and the app's home screen is simply "who wants to read tonight?"

Shared vocabulary: the three-lane model (all alternatives)

Every book a child touches sits in one of three private lanes. Practice: text the child reads successfully with little frustration. Stretch: richer text with adult, peer, or audio support — the right response is suggesting a co-read buddy, never locking the title because a score says it is too hard. Joy: anything the child genuinely wants, including easy books and rereads — in the study's words, "this lane is not a consolation prize; it is central to habit and attachment." Two rules are absolute whichever alternative ships: lanes are chosen by adults and children, never computed as a gate, and they never appear in the child-facing UI as levels; and no reward differs by lane.

AThe Hearth Log — a trust-first family reading log

How it works

The Hearth Log treats the family as the verification system, modeled directly on what actually works in library summer reading programs and BOOK IT!: a simple log, a time-based personal goal, and an adult in the loop as celebrant rather than auditor reading_report_1of2. Logging takes under fifteen seconds and happens after reading — anywhere, any format, no device present during the reading itself. The child opens the app, taps the book they're on (an "active stack" seeded from BookCheckedOut events), and drags a slider: 10, 15, 20, 30, 45+ minutes. Read-alouds, audiobook-plus-text, rereads, and sibling-shared reading all count and are tagged, because the research says everything counts reading_report_1of2. Each log emits ReadingSessionLogged(book, minutes, verification_level: honor).

Verification is a two-tier co-sign. Any log stands on its own at honor level and always produces baseline game value — a child with no engaged parent is never locked out. Once a day, a parent (or older sibling, or librarian at the desk) taps a single "we read" confirmation on a paired device or through a simple grown-up gate on the child's; this upgrades that day's sessions to co_signed, which downstream modules may reward more richly but never punitively withhold. The co-sign is built to feel like sharing: the parent screen shows "Piper read 20 minutes of Dog Man — Grubble the Book Gnome wants to hear about it," nudging the two-minute book conversation the research identifies as more valuable than any prize reading_report_2of2.

Anti-cheese comes from economics, not surveillance. BThe Lap Nest — a session-timer companion

How it works

The Lap Nest makes the reading session itself the core interaction: the child props the device nearby, picks the book they're reading from their stack, taps "start reading," and a creature from their collection climbs into an on-screen nest to listen. The screen then does as little as possible — a soft ambient scene, the creature occasionally turning a page of its own tiny book, no animations that reward looking at it. The design goal is a fireplace, not a slot machine: ambient presence that makes the session feel accompanied, with all visual interest front-loaded at start and end so mid-session glances find nothing new.

The timer measures the session and emits ReadingSessionLogged(book, minutes, verification_level: timed) — higher confidence than an honor log, because the app knows a continuous window elapsed with the device face-up and untouched (touching it pauses the nest: "Puddlewick looked up"). It cannot know the child actually read, so two light corroborations upgrade confidence without ever gating the baseline: a session that opens with BookScanned (a barcode tap of the physical book) logs as timed_scanned — the book demonstrably in hand — and an optional end-of-session "tell the creature one thing," a single tap-choice or 5-second voice note about the story, never graded, stored locally, surfaced to parents as a conversation starter. Kids who skip it lose nothing; kids who do it get a warmer creature reaction.

Session rules follow the research dosage. Minimum creditable session: 10 minutes — below that the creature "settles in" and the nest gently suggests a few more pages. A sweet-spot celebration fires at 15–20 minutes for ages 6–9 and 20–30 for 10–12 (the nest glows once, the creature sighs contentedly), and the session may continue as long as the child wants, minutes counting fully to 60/day of game value reading_report_1of2. Goals are child-selected weekly targets in reading days (default 5-of-7); the streak is the nest itself, a rolling-week nest that gets cozier with each reading day and never degrades below "tidy." Two missed days pause growth; nothing is lost. A "traveling nest" mode (parent toggle) allows honor-logged sessions for car rides and camping, so the timer never becomes a cage.

Anti-cheese: the exploit is starting the timer and walking away, and the countermeasures stay ambient — no mid-session check-ins, no "are you still reading?" popups (that's surveillance). Value simply tops out: sessions cap at 45 creditable minutes, days at 60, and an idle-device session produces exactly what an honest one produces — minutes, at the same rate, capped. The richer outcomes (bond depth, BookFinished confidence) key off the scan and tell-one-thing corroborations a timer-parker won't bother with; faking is possible but pays no better than reading and requires more scheming. Finishing uses the same length-plausible floors as Alternative A; abandoning is the same penalty-free "back to the stack" flow, and the creature who listened to an abandoned book still remembers it fondly — choice is protected reading_report_2of2. Edge cases: shared-tablet households get per-child nest profiles with a picture-passcode; audiobook-plus-text runs the timer with a "listening along" tag; a dead battery mid-session recovers via honor completion ("about how long did you read after Puddlewick fell asleep?").

CBook Trails — per-book quests with creature checkpoints

How it works

Book Trails converts each checked-out book into a small quest map: a trail of 3–6 waypoints generated from the book's metadata (page count, chapter structure, genre headings), walked by that book's not-yet-hatched creature. Reading moves the creature down the trail; finishing the book completes the trail and hatches it. This is the most "gamey" alternative, and its safety depends on strict adherence to the research red lines: waypoints are celebrations, never gates; no waypoint can block reading ahead; and comprehension moments are conversations, never tests reading_report_2of2.

At checkout the trail appears with the creature's egg at the start. The child logs progress by chapter or by session ("read through chapter 4" or a minute log — their choice; both advance the trail proportionally). At each waypoint the trail creature asks the child something it's curious about — one question, dialogue-framed, answerable in one tap from three playful options or skippable outright: after Charlotte's Web chapter 5, the egg murmurs, "I heard there's a spider who can WRITE? What did she write?" — "SOME PIG" / "a grocery list" / "her name." Any answer, wrong or skipped included, advances the trail — but a story-consistent answer bumps the session's verification_level to checkpointed, the quiz-free comprehension signal at the top of the §4 ladder. The tone rules are absolute: the creature is an eager listener who missed the story, not an examiner, and questions target memorable story beats — the AR-critique literature's "literal recall" problem is acceptable here because nothing rides on correctness reading_report_1of2.

Goals and streaks stay time-honest underneath the quest dressing: the weekly goal is still child-chosen reading days or minutes at the age-band floor (15–20 min/day for 6–9; 20–30 for 10–12) reading_report_1of2; trails simply visualize where those minutes went, and multiple trails run in parallel because kids juggle books. Series get trailheads: finishing Magic Tree House #1 reveals a longer path with #2's egg visible in the distance — attacking the "next book" problem the research flags as the durable volume lever, and making series reading (what kids actually choose) the visually rewarded default reading_report_2of2. Trails never regress; an untouched trail's egg naps.

Anti-cheese: the exploit is tapping through chapters without reading. Waypoint questions make blind-tapping detectable but never punishable — a run of story-inconsistent answers just holds the trail at honor confidence, so the hatch is a sleepy one (as in A and B) until a parent co-sign, a rereading pass, or later consistent checkpoints wake it. Chapter-log pacing has plausibility floors too: claiming 12 Hatchet chapters in one evening logs fine but caps that day's creditable advancement at the 60-minute equivalent, the rest completing over subsequent days ("the trail takes time to walk"). Abandoning is a designed moment with real warmth: tap "leave the trail," and a little porter-creature carries the egg back to the trailhead with the line "Not every path is yours. This one keeps." No penalty, no streak damage, resumable forever — abandonment-without-cost is non-negotiable per the choice research reading_report_2of2. Module 5 — Library & Reading Systems: how the game knows about books and borrows

This module is Stacklings' bridge to the real library world: the metadata pipeline that turns an ISBN into genre, subject, and reading-level signals; the mechanism (if any) by which the game learns about real borrowing; digital-lending (Libby/OverDrive) inclusion; and the privacy envelope around all of it. It is the sole producer of BookCheckedOut and BookReturned and the canonical source of book_metadata — the genre spine every other module keys creatures, care needs, and rewards off — and it owns graceful degradation: offline, card-less, or fifty miles from the nearest branch. One rule is non-negotiable across all three alternatives: children's circulation records are legally protected data (library confidentiality statutes in ~48 U.S. states) and children under 13 are COPPA-covered, so every alternative is designed data-minimal first, integration-deep second.

Design tensions. Fidelity versus privacy: the more truthfully we know a kid borrowed a book, the closer we get to holding a child's circulation record — the single most legally sensitive asset this product could touch. Determinism versus metadata mess: "same book → same creature everywhere" collides with the reality that one work has many ISBNs and up to five overlapping genre systems metadata_report §6.5; a canonical work-clustering rule and a genre-precedence rule must be picked and never changed silently. Consumer reach versus B2B sales speed: anything requiring library cooperation (ILS API access, SIP2 credentials) is library-by-library or consortium-by-consortium procurement — years, not quarters lending_systems_overview §5, §6. Digital lending is gated: OverDrive/Libby API access is "a business-development question, not a technical one" and exists at OverDrive's pleasure, so no alternative may make it load-bearing lending_systems_overview §2.1. And equity: rewarding verified borrowing more than reading any book structurally advantages kids near well-funded branches — the executive must decide how much verification premium is acceptable.

Digital parity (all alternatives)

The manuscript's digital story is thin — a Libby deep-link and self-report at Library B, "audiobooks log as sessions if supported" at Loop C — and the second design study fills the gap with three alternative-neutral rules, all consistent with the OverDrive-never-load-bearing constraint. First, same work, same creature, across formats: a share sheet, catalog link, or title search opens a "digital portal" that reveals the same base creature as the print work — which quietly commits the determinism substrate to work-level clustering spanning print, ebook, and audio, feeding workshop question 2. Second, Quiet Check-ins: digital sessions log only broad self-selected states — started, continued, paused, finished — and reading days, never continuous screen surveillance; device-specific page counts are never a currency. Third, digital provenance is a cosmetic — a glow, drift, or portal mark, alongside the Echo trait for audio — never weaker or stronger progression. The study's own validation gate is worth keeping: digital-first cohorts should not feel like a fallback audience.

AThe Field Guide — scan and metadata only, zero integration

How it works

The game never talks to a library system. Its entire connection to the library world is a barcode scanner and a metadata service we own — and that turns out to be almost enough, because the metadata is the magic, and the metadata is substantially open.

The pipeline. A kid points the device at any book's ISBN barcode (or types the ISBN, or photographs the cover as fallback). The scan emits BookScanned(isbn) and queries the Stacklings Metadata Service, an aggregation layer we build and cache aggressively. Its sources, in precedence order: (1) Library of Congress CYAC/CIP records — most U.S. children's publishers participate in CIP, so LC creates high-quality juvenile records with summaries, Children's Subject Headings, and LCGFT genre terms before publication metadata_report §6.1, harvested via LC's open bulk data and id.loc.gov authority files; (2) union-catalog lookups via Z39.50/SRU, the venerable open MARC search protocols, plus WorldCat's member/partner APIs if we join OCLC as a partner lending_systems_overview §3.3–3.4; (3) publisher-side BISAC codes (JUV/JNF/YAF sections) from ONIX feeds as fallback for brand-new or small-press titles. The service normalizes everything into one book_metadata object: canonical work ID, title, author, genre and subject facets, audience band, page count, and a determinism seed.

Determinism. Editions cluster to a canonical work (normalized title+author, cross-checked against LC/OCLC work identifiers), so the paperback, hardcover, and library-bound Dog Man all hatch the same creature; the creature seed is a hash of the work ID — Pillar 2 satisfied with zero library involvement. The genre precedence rule — the shared vocabulary the whole game consumes — is fixed and versioned so determinism holds: MARC 655 LCGFT terms first ("what the book is" — the very question a creature's element answers) metadata_report §3.2; then 650 second-indicator-1 Children's Subject Headings for traits and flavor ("what it's about": Dragons, Horses, Separation) metadata_report §6.2; then the BISAC JUV family; then DDC class for nonfiction.

Borrowing is inferred, honestly. With no card link, BookCheckedOut is emitted when a kid scans a book and taps "I borrowed this!" — carrying provenance self_reported (§4). BookReturned(book, on_time) fires when they scan it again at "goodbye" time, with on_time computed against a family-set 3-week "library rhythm" timer rather than real due dates; downstream modules must treat these as low-verification and cap rewards accordingly — the §4 ladder handles this. A library's 14-digit Codabar item sticker often covers the ISBN barcode; the scanner handles both, but an item barcode alone can't identify a title without ILS access — an honest limitation surfaced in UX ("find the other barcode — it starts with 978!").

Receipt verification — the middle rung. Between self_reported and ils_verified sits a rung the second design study contributes: scanning the QR code on a library checkout receipt, entering a rotating branch event code, or tapping a staff token upgrades a borrow to receipt_verified — the token proves an event happened, not identity or history, with zero ILS integration and zero patron data. It is the cheapest honest upgrade to the return loop this alternative can buy, available years before any consortium contract signs. One honest caveat: receipt formats vary by ILS and many receipts carry no QR at all, so the flow needs a graceful typed fallback (a short code, or date plus branch) and must never make the desk moment feel like a checkpoint.

Privacy and offline. Best-in-class by construction: no patron identity, no library account, no circulation record ever exists; scan history stays on-device; the server sees ISBN lookups tied to a pseudonymous device token, retained only in aggregate; the COPPA surface is a parent email for account recovery. Offline, a bundled pack — the top ~200,000 children's ISBNs with compressed metadata (a few hundred MB, or a 50k starter tier) — resolves scans instantly in a basement, a bookmobile, or a no-data household; unknown ISBNs queue and resolve on reconnect behind a Mystery Egg placeholder that hatches once metadata arrives. The pipeline works identically for owned books, school-library books, little-free-library finds, and grandma's shelf — the no-card kid loses nothing.

BCard & Companion — opt-in library-card linking via ILS APIs and SIP2

How it works

Everything in Alternative A remains the floor — the scanner, the metadata service, the offline pack. B adds an opt-in layer: a parent links the family's library card, and real borrowing starts flowing into the game as verified events.

The plumbing. Physical-side integration is the open door in this ecosystem: nearly every ILS speaks SIP2, and the major vendors expose REST APIs — Sierra API, Polaris PAPI, Symphony Web Services, Koha and Evergreen's open APIs — capable of patron auth and checkout data, provided the library enables access lending_systems_overview §3.3. That proviso is the business model: we onboard library systems one at a time — or, far better, one consortium at a time, since winning a state consortium can mean hundreds of libraries at once lending_systems_overview §6.5 — each granting Stacklings API or SIP2 credentials. We build an ILS-agnostic adapter layer, the same abstraction BiblioCommons, Aspen, and Capira each built internally lending_systems_overview §3.2–3.3, starting with the open-source ILSes (Koha, Evergreen) where nothing gates us. In-app, a kid's home library is either "Linked" (partner library, real events) or "Field Guide" (Alternative A behavior) — the map colors in as systems sign.

The flow. A parent selects their library and authenticates with card number + PIN through the library's own mechanism (SIP2 patron-status or a vendor auth endpoint — there is no universal library identity layer, so this is per-ILS work lending_systems_overview §5). A sync job then polls the linked account's current-checkouts list: a new item resolves to ISBN via the ILS bib record → BookCheckedOut(book_metadata) at provenance ils_verified; an item disappearing from the list → BookReturned(book, on_time) computed against the real due date. Real due dates make the loop module's appointment hooks honest: "Salsawyrm's book flies home in 3 days!"

Holds and renewals — the appointment nobody built. The sync that sees checkouts also sees holds and renewals, and these become the two optional §4 events. HoldPlaced(book, coarse_status) turns the library's most frustrating kid-facing moment into an appointment: placing a hold spawns a traveling message egg in the habitat whose route advances with coarse status changes — waiting becomes anticipation rather than a dead end, and arrival day is a library trip with a name on it. LoanRenewed(book, new_due) quietly reschedules every due-date trigger downstream (the due-date save below shows the manual version this event automates). Both are proposals from the second design study, and consumers must degrade gracefully where they never fire, since only linked libraries can emit them. A worked example: Theo finishes Wings of Fire: The Dragonet Prophecy and the sequel is checked out — instead of a dead end, his hold on The Lost Heir creates the egg, its route ticks forward when the copy comes back in transit, and Tidewhistle's arrival is an event he has been watching travel toward him for nine days.

Privacy architecture — the hard part. A linked child's checkout list is a children's circulation record, protected by state confidentiality statutes and, because our users are under 13, squarely inside COPPA. Non-negotiable rules: (1) verifiable parental consent before any linking — the card credentials are the parent's to give, and the COPPA VPC flow gates it; (2) transform-and-discard — the sync service converts checkouts to game events and derived genre facets, then discards the title-level record; we retain no server-side borrowing history, only deliberately one-way creature outcomes ("hatched from a Humorous-fiction picture book"); (3) credentials tokenized and vaulted, never stored client-side; (4) no third-party data sharing, ever, plus a one-tap parent "unlink and purge"; (5) our library contracts explicitly bind us as the library's confidentiality agent — libraries will demand this anyway, and offering it unprompted is a sales asset.

Digital lending and degradation. Libby/OverDrive checkouts flow in only where OverDrive grants partner API access and the library activates it — historically restricted, tightening, and never load-bearing lending_systems_overview §2.1, §5 Path C. Default: deep-link out to Libby for discovery and accept a self-reported "I borrowed it in Libby" (scan the cover on-screen) at Alternative A fidelity; where a Palace Project library exists, its open OPDS/ODL stack legitimately surfaces digital availability lending_systems_overview §2.5; Hoopla is closed and not attempted lending_systems_overview §2.2. One sentence of future-proofing from the second design study: an interlibrary-loan signal, when the plumbing someday exists, can mark a creature Faraway — a travel trunk and route tags, provenance and lore rather than power. Sync is server-side, so the child's device needs no connectivity for events to accrue — they land on next open. Unlinked or un-partnered kids get the complete Alternative A game: linking upgrades verification and the honesty of the return loop, and never gates content or creatures (Pillar 1: no premium tier of any kind).

CBranch & Bramble — the library as an in-game venue

How it works

Alternative C contains all of B (which contains all of A) and adds the layer that makes the library itself a place in the game: branch check-ins, librarian-curated quests, and summer-reading tie-ins, delivered through a formal partnership program with staff-facing tools. This is Pillar 1 at full strength — the library as input, venue, and partner — and it makes Stacklings a genuine B2B2C product, with a librarian dashboard and program operations layered over B's plumbing.

Branches as habitats. Each partner branch becomes a named in-game location — a "Grove" — with its own ambient habitat visible to kids who check in there. Check-in is deliberately low-tech and offline-tolerant: a framed poster at the desk with a weekly-rotated, cryptographically signed QR code (a photo of last month's poster fails; validation runs on-device offline and syncs later). No beacons and no geofencing of children — location inference on under-13s is a COPPA hazard refused outright; the QR proves presence without tracking movement. A check-in emits a venue-scoped PlayerReturnTrigger context and unlocks that Grove's seasonal content. Kids who never visit any branch lose venue flavor only — never creatures, never core progression.

Librarian-curated quests. The staff dashboard (web, no install, authenticated per branch) lets a children's librarian assemble a "Quest Shelf": pick 6–12 titles by ISBN, name the quest, set a window. The metadata service resolves the titles' shared facets and auto-generates the quest's creature theme — the librarian curates books, the game does game design. Completing a quest (scan or verified checkout of N titles plus BookFinished from the gamification module) earns a Grove-exclusive cosmetic habitat item. The dashboard points genrefication energy at a game: the same librarian judgment that puts a green Mystery sticker on a spine now stocks a quest shelf, and the documented result of genre-forward curation — kids find books faster and circulation rises — is the outcome the partnership sells metadata_report §6.4, AASL study. (The second design study proposed an equivalent branch-encounter capture layer — genre zones, QR totems, scavenger clues, explicitly "on top of another capture model"; it is this same mechanic, and this alternative's signed-QR, no-geofencing treatment is the stronger privacy posture, so nothing further needed importing.)

Summer reading tie-in. Summer reading is the library world's biggest annual kids' engagement push. The dashboard's "program mode" maps a library's existing summer-reading milestones onto Stacklings quests, and we export back aggregate, de-identified participation counts — never per-child records — for the library's state-library reporting. Integration with whatever tracking software the library already runs is CSV-in/CSV-out by design: no dependency on any summer-reading vendor.

Partnership structure and privacy. Consortia are the leverage point and sales cycles are municipal-slow lending_systems_overview §6.1, §6.5, so the program has exactly two motions: (1) state/regional consortium agreements bundling B's data plumbing (SIP2/ILS API credentials granted consortium-wide) with the venue program; (2) a lightweight "Seedling Branch" tier any single library can self-serve join — dashboard, QR poster, and quests only, no ILS integration — so an enthusiastic librarian anywhere can participate the week she hears about us, at Alternative A data fidelity. Pricing to libraries: free or at-cost. Library budgets are flat and we extract no rent (Pillar 1); the program's value to us is verification, distribution, and legitimacy, and the grant/foundation ecosystem around youth literacy is the realistic revenue conversation for this tier. Privacy is B's regime plus: librarians see only aggregates (quest completion counts, anonymous Grove activity), never individual children's reading; check-in data is venue-scoped presence, not location history; program exports are k-anonymized (cells under 10 suppressed). Librarians are privacy professionals — the dashboard's restraint is a feature they will audit and then evangelize.

Pilot: 10 branches, one summer. ☙ Librarian’s Note — Recommendation Signal

The research points toward a staged composite rather than a bet: the metadata layer (A) is buildable today on open, stable infrastructure — LC/CYAC records, the LCGFT/CYAC/BISAC vocabularies, Z39.50/SRU — with no gatekeepers metadata_report §1.2, §6.1; lending_systems_overview §3.3, while both integration layers ride the physical-side protocols the lending research identifies as the ecosystem's genuinely open surface (SIP2, vendor and open-source ILS APIs) and its stated leverage point, consortia lending_systems_overview §5 Path A, §6.5. The same research warns explicitly against making OverDrive/Libby load-bearing and against underestimating municipal sales cycles — which argues for shipping A nationally while B/C pilots run in one or two consortia. The question the executive must actually decide is the verification premium: how much better the game treats an ils_verified borrow than a scanned family-bookshelf book — a choice that trades event fidelity against equity, and against how much legally protected children's circulation data this company is willing to hold. The receipt_verified rung softens the dilemma at the low end: it upgrades borrow fidelity everywhere, immediately, at Library A's privacy posture.

Acceptance criteria (any alternative)

  1. Disabling every partner integration leaves a coherent and enjoyable core game.
  2. No library credentials are stored in the game client or exposed to any other player.
  3. A partner can choose category-only data and still support checkout, return, and branch mechanics.
  4. Every vendor adapter maps to the same canonical event vocabulary (§4); no partner quirk leaks into game rules.
  5. No strategic power or permanent rarity depends on privilege, geography, or vendor access; branch exclusives become discoverable elsewhere after a window, or stay purely commemorative.
  6. Any public or branch-facing surface runs on prewritten reactions, with no direct child-to-child messaging.

Module 6 (optional) — Shared Play: rooms without races

This module is the merge's largest addition. The five source modules contain no social design at all — the Vision only says what the game is not — and the second design study supplies a complete asynchronous-cooperative layer that turns out to be fully compatible with that refusal: no feeds, no chat, no PII exposure. The whole module is optional; a build that ships nothing from it loses nothing from Modules 1–5. Its spine is the ContributionReceiptIssued event (§4): every eligible action — a shared read, a long solo session, a branch visit, a checkout — yields exactly one receipt, normalized to value 1 and never expandable back into the reading or library event beneath it. That one number is the module's whole privacy argument, because it makes shared progress structurally incapable of becoming a reading-volume leaderboard. Four principles govern every alternative: asynchronous by default (no child must be online at the same time as a stranger), cooperative not comparative (milestones and discoveries, never rankings of pages, minutes, books, or "best reader"), expressive not exposing (players share a creature, an object, or a deliberately chosen recommendation — never ambient reading history), and useful alone (every collaborative reward is cosmetic, narrative, or discovery-oriented; a solo player can still collect every family and finish every route).

Two of the study's five flows are handled elsewhere rather than presented as alternatives: the Co-located Shelf Safari (hosted event rooms, team book hunts, no speed rewards) folds into Library C's event program as one more thing a Grove can host, and Curator Circles — an adult and older-teen exhibit mode — is deferred, because adult players sit outside this manuscript's 6–12 remit and inside workshop question 11.

Design tensions. The study ships its flows as a fixed rollout sequence (household, then branch, then friends); this manuscript recasts the sequence as decisions. How much social surface at v1: the three alternatives below add moderation and safety surface in strict order — the Household Shelf has almost none, Branch Restoration adds staff tooling and a public aggregate display, Expeditions add a friend graph with guardian approval, blocking, reporting, and adult/child graph separation — and the workshop must decide how far down that list v1 goes (question 12). Warmth versus exposure: every shared surface is one design slip from leaking reading history; the receipt's non-reversibility and the boundaries table in Appendix B are the containment, and any new shared feature must pass both. Obligation risk: a shared space can curdle into a chore, so the study's healthy-failure rule is adopted game-wide — milestones require a small number of distinct contributions, never 100% participation, and absent players are never listed as missing. And social pull versus safety: the study's own risk register concedes that maximally safe multiplayer can be socially weak (Appendix E, #8); the counter it proposes is making collaboration produce something impossible alone, not adding chat.

AThe Household Shared Shelf

How it works

A guardian creates the Shelf: one magical room shared by household profiles — siblings, cousins on a family account, a classroom household. Each profile keeps its own private collection; the room is a display and cooperation surface, never a merged account. The group picks one of three low-pressure prompts ("Night Visitors," "Things That Fly," "Books We Reread"), and each member may place one contribution: a creature visit, a decoration, or a deliberately shared recommendation. Contributed creatures interact — a hybrid animation, a temporary visitor drawn by the combined traits — and the room mints a weekly postcard or family emblem. Participation is optional and never equalized by reading volume: the room shows what was placed, not who read how much. Milestones need a small number of distinct contributions, and a sibling who placed nothing is simply not mentioned — there is no missing-persons list. The privacy floor: contributions are explicit acts; reading totals, titles (unless deliberately shared), timers, and private notes never surface in the room.

Worked examples

"Things That Fly." Maya and Theo's household picks the prompt. Maya contributes Salsawyrm (Dragons Love Tacos), who technically qualifies and knows it; Theo contributes Emberquill (Wings of Fire: The Dragonet Prophecy). Mom, who read nothing this week, is not listed as absent, because there is no list.

When she accepts it weeks later, the room mints a paired keepsake. Nothing about Theo's reading pace or Maya's response time is displayed anywhere. In §8's terms, the card is one "familiar continuation" slot of the next-book menu, handed across profiles.

BBranch Restoration

How it works

A librarian opens a themed project at a partner branch — one room of a magical branch archive to restore, with dates, allowed contribution types, and three to five visual milestones. Any eligible action — a checkout, a completed reading goal, a branch QR, event attendance, a curated-trail scan — earns the player one receipt, which they choose whether to deposit as an anonymous Spark. The branch sees one Spark; it cannot see or reconstruct the title, minutes, or mode beneath it. At 25, 50, 75, and 100 percent the room transforms: shelves return, lights come on, visitors arrive, new book trails open for everyone. The project closes with a permanent season snapshot for the branch, and every contributor receives the same core keepsake, plus a private mark for the milestones they witnessed. The anti-competition rule is absolute: show aggregate progress and milestone moments, never a personal contribution leaderboard.

How it works

028.9 SHFMODULE 7

Module 7 (optional) — Story Shelf & Book Personality: the reader behind the creatures

This module gives Stacklings a durable memory of the player's reading life: a child-safe, creature-shaped equivalent of Letterboxd or Goodreads. The Story Shelf shows every book the player has scanned, borrowed, started, finished, saved, or added manually; lets them rate or react to a title without turning reading into a review assignment; and builds a simple, explainable Book Personality from the patterns in that history. That profile is useful even in a solo game — it can tune next-book menus, quizzes, collection goals, room decorations, and game prompts — but its largest strategic value is multiplayer: taste becomes a reason for another Stackling to visit the player's care home carrying a book recommendation.

The module is optional at the contract level but foundational at the product level. Modules 1–5 function without it, and a privacy-maximal account may turn it off or delete it completely. When present, it listens to BookScanned, BookCheckedOut, ReadingSessionLogged, BookFinished, and BookReturned and reconciles them into one work-level shelf entry. It never silently equates appeared in the app with read: scanned, borrowed, reading, finished, paused, and saved-for-later are distinct states. Nor does adding a book manually mint a creature or a game reward. The shelf may remember owned books, classroom books, ebooks, audiobooks, and books read before the account existed; the capture module remains the authority on whether a library-book interaction creates a Stackling. The tracker is broader than the collection, but it cannot counterfeit the collection.

Design tensions. Memory versus minimization: a useful reading diary wants title-level history, while Appendix B treats that history as sensitive child data. The answer must be explicit modes, local-first storage where feasible, family-readable deletion, and private-by-default entries — not a buried consent checkbox. Expression versus homework: a five-star control is quick, but reviews, reading targets, and completion pressure can make an abandoned book feel like failure; the design therefore accepts “not for me,” rereads, partial reads, listening, and no rating at all as first-class outcomes. Personalization versus labeling: “you like funny mysteries” can invite discovery, while “you are a Level 3 fantasy reader” can freeze identity and imply ability; Book Personality must describe recent patterns, show its ingredients, and change visibly as the shelf changes. Finally, social discovery versus stranger exposure: taste-matched visits are the feature with the greatest upside and the greatest safety surface, so the three alternatives below are cumulative sharing ceilings — A is private, B adds known people, C adds anonymized taste neighbors — rather than three incompatible data models.

Common foundation: the Story Shelf (all alternatives)

One book, one evolving card. Every entry resolves to a work-level token where metadata permits, so a paperback scan, an audiobook checkout, and a manual title search do not create three copies of the same work. The card shows cover, title, author, format, source, dates at coarse day precision, associated Stackling if one exists, and one of six player-controlled states: Want to Read, On My Stack, Reading, Finished, Paused, or Not for Me. Automatic events may suggest a state — checkout suggests On My Stack; a session suggests Reading; BookFinished suggests Finished — but the child can correct it, and a return never automatically means finished.

Manual additions. “Add a book” supports ISBN scan, title/author search, voice search, and a minimal fallback form for an unlisted title. The player chooses what happened: read before Stacklings, reading now, want to read, or not for me. Metadata reconciliation runs later without overwriting the player's rating or state. A manually added title contributes to Book Personality once matched to enough metadata, but it carries source: manual and cannot emit CreatureAcquired, satisfy a verified borrow, or unlock provenance-gated rarity. If the same title is later scanned or borrowed, the existing card gains that source and its creature association rather than duplicating.

Ratings and reactions. Rating is optional and fast: one to five stars for Curators, or a presentation-profile translation for younger players — “not for me,” “okay,” “liked it,” “loved it,” “new favorite.” A second optional row of tags captures why: funny, cozy, surprising, exciting, beautiful, taught me something, great characters, read-aloud favorite, too scary, too slow, or want more. Tags are structured; private notes and voice reflections stay private and never enter matching. “Not for me” is a useful preference signal, not a failed quest, and removing a rating has no cost.

Book Personality. The system maintains a small facet vector from the shelf's matched metadata and the player's reactions. The child sees no opaque score. They see a living breakdown such as 35% Giggle, 25% Shade, 20% Cog, 20% everything else; “you often choose graphic books and series”; and “lately: mysteries with jokes.” The inputs are limited to broad, child-legible dimensions: the game-wide elements/genres, fiction versus nonfiction, format, series versus standalone, and a small editorial tone vocabulary such as cozy, funny, adventurous, puzzling, factual, or lyrical. Each finished title contributes one count; a sampled or paused title contributes half a count; a four- or five-star rating adds half a preference count to its facets; a one- or two-star rating informs “less often chosen” suggestions but never becomes a negative identity label. Recent reading receives a modest recency weight so the portrait can change, while an “all time / lately” toggle makes that change explainable.

The playful label on top — for example Curious Questmaker, Cozy Creature Friend, or Laughing Puzzle Hunter — is assembled from the two strongest broad facets and is always secondary to the breakdown. Confidence is shown as just sprouting (3–7 matched books), taking shape (8–19), or well stocked (20+), not as a psychometric claim. The player can hide a title from the calculation, reset the portrait, or choose “surprise me” so recommendations do not collapse into more of the same. Sensitive-title mode excludes protected titles and categories from personality, matching, and social display by default.

CWandering Recommenders — anonymized visits from taste neighbors

How it works

Alternative C includes A and B, then adds the user's multiplayer idea: occasionally, a Stackling from a reader with similar tastes visits the care home and recommends a book its player deliberately placed in a recommendation pool. The visitor is the creature, not a browsable child profile. The card explains the match in broad language — “Your shelves both enjoy funny mysteries” — and offers Save, Not for Me, or No More Visitors Like This. It never shows a username, age, branch, exact similarity percentage, full shelf, rating history, or direct-contact affordance.

Matching runs only after the receiving account opts in and reaches at least eight matched, non-sensitive books. It compares a coarse, normalized facet vector — elements, format, series preference, and tone — after sensitive facets, rare categories, timestamps, ability proxies, and exact titles are removed. Candidates must come from a sufficiently large eligible pool; the service selects from a k-anonymous neighborhood rather than the single mathematically closest child, rotates matches to avoid reconstructing a profile over time, caps visits at two per week, and keeps no persistent stranger graph. Recommendation inventory is also explicit: a book enters the pool only when its reader taps “Let my Stackling recommend this,” and the book must pass age-band, metadata-confidence, sensitive-title, and moderation checks. The receiving player can turn the feature off, block a title/facet/visitor token, or clear their matching vector without deleting the private shelf.

310.2 METAPPENDIX D

Appendix D: Pilot and measurement plan (build-agnostic), with pre-committed kill criteria

Adapted from the second design study. Its phases assumed its own recommended bundle, so they are recast against "the chosen build," and its age language is aligned to this manuscript's 6–12 remit. Build 1's sentence — "Hearthlight is the honest baseline any richer build should have to beat in a pilot" — becomes a measurement design here.

Phases.

  • Phase 0 — paper and clickable prototype. Test the scan reveal, creature appeal, and whether the product reads as a game rather than an assignment. No live library data.
  • Phase 1 — vertical slice of the chosen build. Mobile scan, offline reveal, roughly twelve families, one habitat, the build's reading-goal and care loop; a Private Story Shelf with scan seeding, manual add, states, reactions, and a clearly labeled “just sprouting” personality; if Module 6 ships, a two-profile Household Shelf on one device.
  • Phase 2 — branch-aware pilot. One to three branches: rotating QR, receipt confirmation, curated trails, and one Branch Restoration if chosen. Test trusted recommendation cards only inside guardian-managed pilot groups.
  • Phase 3 — one ILS integration. Checkout, return, renewal, and hold events through a single partner adapter; measure staff burden, event accuracy, shelf reconciliation, and the incremental value beyond receipt verification.
  • Phase 4 — consortium, open digital (Palace/OPDS), and limited taste-neighbor pilot. Only after the physical loop shows sustained child demand and Module 7's privacy, anonymity, moderation, and recommendation-quality gates pass.

Include / cut for the prototype. Include: six to twelve creature families, ten example metadata mappings, instant local reveal with delayed enrichment, one reading goal and one care outcome, one next-book path, shared-device child profiles, and a small user-entered Story Shelf sufficient to test states, reactions, and whether children understand the Book Personality breakdown. Cut: combat, breeding, trading, open chat, searchable profiles, generative creature art, a large narrative campaign, bulk title-history import, stranger matching — and monetization of any kind, which Pillar 1 removes permanently rather than provisionally.

Metrics, each with its interpretation caution:

Question Metric Caution
Would players choose it unassigned? Voluntary replay intent, return sessions, "would you play at home?" Adult approval and novelty are not substitutes for player pull
Does capture feel magical? Reveal completion; the child's explanation of "why this creature"; desire to scan another Repeated scanning alone may show novelty, not reading intent
Does it support reading? Goal starts and completions, book resumption, caregiver observation Do not claim causal reading gains from app telemetry alone
Does it solve next-book discovery? Recommendation opens, deep links, holds, follow-on scans Availability and family logistics can block action even when interest exists

Is it equitable? Completion by device mode, age band, access path, branch Do not infer sensitive attributes beyond what evaluation genuinely needs

Pre-committed success thresholds — set before launch, not after: a majority of the pilot cohort would replay voluntarily without an adult requiring it; a majority can explain the scan → creature relationship after one use; a reading goal starts in three taps or fewer and is skippable without losing the creature; at least one-third of active users follow a next-book path or scan a related title; a majority of Story Shelf testers can add or correct a book and explain at least two personality facets without calling them levels; staff time stays within a pre-agreed weekly budget; and no critical flow requires a child-owned phone, cloud-synced title history, taste matching, or a public profile.

Pre-committed kill criteria. These buy one discipline: the team can call a pilot unsuccessful even when the anecdotes are positive. Narrow, reposition, or stop if several of the following remain true after focused iteration:

  1. Players return mainly to scan and reveal, but do not choose to play with creatures they already own.
  2. Children cannot explain why generated creatures belong to their books in a specific, satisfying way.
  3. Engagement concentrates among already-enthusiastic readers, with no credible bridge for hesitant ones.
  4. The digital path feels administrative, second-class, or hostage to integrations that never sign.
  5. Safe shared play creates no reason to invite or return with another player.
  6. Library participation requires recurring staff effort beyond the pre-committed budget.
  7. No funding model consistent with Pillar 1 — grants, consortium or program licensing, foundation support — covers the honest bottom-up cost of art, metadata, safety, accessibility, and moderation. (The study's version of this criterion contemplated a paid family subscription; Pillar 1 forecloses that, so the criterion is rewritten to the funding models that remain.)
  8. The Story Shelf feels like recordkeeping homework, Book Personality is repeatedly understood as a reading level or fixed label, or users cannot reliably correct and delete the data behind it.
  9. Wandering Recommenders cannot meet the anonymity threshold without weak matches, repeatedly surface sensitive or inappropriate titles, or produce less discovery breadth than the non-personalized wildcard menu.

Four gate tests join Phases 0–1. The no-new-book test: give players a fixed collection and observe whether they voluntarily start another session, change a plan, or pursue a self-chosen goal. The semantic-fit test: show kids books and their generated creatures without explaining the rules; they should articulate a plausible, emotionally satisfying connection beyond broad genre or cover color. The personality-legibility test: after viewing the breakdown, a child can name the shelf evidence behind it and understands that reading something different will change it. The privacy-control test: child and guardian can independently find how to hide a title, turn off matching, and delete shelf history, then accurately describe what another player can and cannot see.
